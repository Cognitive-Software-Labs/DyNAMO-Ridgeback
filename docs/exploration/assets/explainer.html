Ridgeback · lab project

Map a building by itself, then find who is in it

A Clearpath Ridgeback drives into rooms it has never seen, builds a map as it goes, and reports where a target, a person or a humanoid robot, stands relative to it. The same robot software runs in two simulators and on the real robot.

How it explores

Five steps, each waits until the one before is ready

Nothing starts on a timer. Each step starts only once the data it needs is actually arriving, so a slow simulator or a late sensor never leaves a step running blind.

SENSE

Sensors and odometry

Lidar scans, the depth camera and wheel odometry, the same on every backend.

MAP

Build the map

SLAM turns lidar scans into a map at 5 cm per cell and keeps track of where the robot is on it.

waits for scans + odometry
PLAN

Plan and drive

Nav2 plans paths and drives the omnidirectional base, up to 0.5 m/s forward or sideways.

waits for the map
CHOOSE

Pick the next goal

The explorer finds the edges of the known map and sends the robot to the best one.

waits for the cost map
FINISH

Stop when done

When no edge of unknown space is left, the robot reports that exploration is complete.

Choosing where to go

Drive to the edge of what is known

A frontier is a known-free cell that touches unknown space. Neighbouring frontier cells are grouped into one frontier, and the robot drives a little past its middle to look into the unknown.

1
Close ones first. Any frontier within 7.2 m: go to the nearest.
2
Otherwise score them. Closeness counts 70 %, size 30 %.
×
Ignore crumbs. Frontiers smaller than 5 cells are dropped.
×
Skip unsafe goals. A goal next to an obstacle is rejected.
×
Give up on stuck goals. Blacklisted after 3 failed tries; cancelled after 60 s without progress.
Finding the target

Name it, box it, measure how far it is

An open-vocabulary detector finds the target from a text label, such as "humanoid robot" or "person", so no target-specific training is needed. Four interchangeable methods then turn the detection into a position: sideways, forward and straight-line distance from the robot's front.

Point cloud

Read the 3D points that fall inside the detection box. Simulator only for now.

Projective ranging

Take the typical depth of the target's pixels and project its centre into 3D.

Euclidean reconstruction

Turn every target pixel into a 3D point, drop the stray ones, take the centre.

Polar profiling

Use the lidar beams that hit the target in the image. Needs no depth camera, but cannot see height.

Box or outline. The target region is either the detector's box or a tight outline from a small segmentation model.
Real or estimated depth. Depth comes from the stereo camera or from a monocular depth network.
A miss stays a miss. The methods are never blended and never fill in for each other, so each one's failures stay visible.

How accurate

Scored against the simulator's ground truth

A benchmark places humanoid targets in scripted scenes, with occlusion, clutter and targets at the image edge, and scores every method against the true position. The same scene file runs in both simulators.

The one published result: polar profiling. Its typical error is under 8 cm, but four trials latched onto a wall instead of the target and carry 72 % of all error. Without them the 95th-percentile error falls from 1.36 m to 0.73 m.

One validation run, 94 scored trials. All benchmark results predate the latest scene re-certification and wait for a rerun; the other three methods have no published numbers yet.
